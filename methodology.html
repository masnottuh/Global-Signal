US Economic Health Index methodology

Last updated: November 20, 2026

Global Signal publishes a monthly US Economic Health Index built only from official US government statistics. This page describes how the index is constructed, what it is compared with historically, and where the data have gaps. It is a description of a transparent arithmetic procedure, not a forecast and not investment advice.

Sources

Six official series enter the index, each from the agency that produces it.

The prototype uses the keyless BLS v1 API, the official CPI flat file, the BEA vintage-history workbook, and the Federal Reserve Board’s Data Download files. Vintages

Every reading is tied to an observation month and to the vintage of each input as published at the time we retrieve it. Official data are revised. When a figure is still an advance, preliminary, or otherwise footnoted estimate, that label is kept with the reading. Real GDP is quarterly and is assigned only to the three months of a published quarter. Mid-quarter months therefore use five of six series by design; the latest official GDP print is shown as context and is not a missing input for that month’s composite. A later revision of an already-published quarter does not add the new quarter’s GDP to a mid-quarter reading.

As of the November 2026 report, October 2026 is a month in 2026Q4, so the composite uses five of six series by design. The latest official GDP print is the 2026Q3 advance estimate from BEA’s vintage-history file dated October 29, 2026; that print, and BEA’s November 25 second estimate if it revises 2026Q3, are context only. October 2026 nonfarm payrolls are marked preliminary in the BLS file.

From changes to a 0–100 index

  1. Compute a simple change for each series, as in the table above.
  2. Apply a documented sign. GDP growth, payroll gains, and industrial production enter positively. Rising unemployment, faster CPI growth, and a rising federal funds rate enter negatively.
  3. Convert each signed change to a z-score against a rolling 120-month (10-year) calendar window, requiring at least 60 available months. A high z-score means the latest change was strong relative to that window, not that the economy is strong in an absolute sense.
  4. Take an equal-weight average of the available z-scores, requiring at least four of six components. If a series has not been published for that month, or is not available that far back, weights are renormalized over the series that are present.
  5. Map the average through the standard normal cumulative distribution and multiply by 100. A reading of 50 means the composite change is in line with its trailing 10-year average of changes.

Regimes

  • Expansion: index 60 or higher
  • Slowdown: index from 40 up to, but not including, 60
  • Contraction: index below 40

Data gaps

A 3-month or 12-month change is marked unavailable if any official observation in its window is missing. Missing months are kept as gaps. They are not interpolated, forward-filled, or dropped so that a change would be computed by skipping over them. The same calendar window is used for rolling z-scores.

After the 2025 lapse in appropriations, BLS did not publish an all-items CPI-U index for October 2025 (series CUSR0000SA0, footnote X) and did not publish a CPS unemployment rate for October 2025 (series LNS14000000, footnote 9). BLS also does not publish net or percent changes that require those observations. In particular, there is no official 12-month all-items CPI rate for October 2026. Nonfarm payrolls for October 2025 were published. The October 2026 3-month CPI change remains defined, because July through October 2026 are complete. See BLS handling of missing data and the revised release dates after the 2025 and 2026 lapses in appropriations.

Historical alignment with NBER recessions

We compare the finished index with official NBER recession months as a contemporaneous alignment check. Official monthly and quarterly data arrive with publication lags and are revised. NBER recession dates are set after the fact. These figures are not a forecast backtest, are not an accuracy rate, and should not be used in marketing. Recession dates are from the NBER business-cycle chronology.

Not investment advice

The index and the monthly report are impersonal summaries of publicly available official statistics and a transparent arithmetic index built from them. They are for information only. They are not financial, investment, legal, or tax advice, are not a recommendation to buy or sell any security or to take any particular action, and are not tailored to any person’s circumstances. Official data are revised. Past readings of this index, and its historical alignment with NBER dates, do not predict future economic or market outcomes.
